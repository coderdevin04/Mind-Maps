# Chemical-Bonding

#### Based on the provided transcripts, here is an ultra-detailed, multi-level hierarchical outline of Chemical Bonding.
## I. Fundamental Concepts of Chemical Bonding
### A. Definition and Nature of the Bond
#### Concept of Connection
##### a.  A chemical bond is defined as a force of attraction that holds two or more chemical species (atoms, ions, molecules) together
##### b.  The fundamental purpose is to connect separate species into a single unit or group
##### c.  Mere attraction without forming a unit (like between Xenon atoms) is not always termed a chemical bond in the classical sense
#### Energy and Stability Relationship
##### a.  Inverse Relationship: When energy is high, stability is low; when energy decreases, stability increases
##### b.  Exothermic Process: Bond formation is an exothermic process where energy is released to the surroundings
##### c.  Analogy: A person running (high energy) is unstable; a person sitting/resting (low energy) is stable
#### Potential Energy Curve and Forces
##### a.  Forces at Play: As atoms approach, both attractive forces (nucleus-electron) and repulsive forces (nucleus-nucleus, electron-electron) operate
##### b.  Dominance of Attraction: Initially, attraction dominates, leading to a decrease in potential energy as atoms approach from infinity
##### c.  Equilibrium Point: At a specific internuclear distance, attraction equals repulsion, energy is minimum, and stability is maximum (Bond Formation)
##### d.  Dominance of Repulsion: If atoms move closer than the equilibrium distance, repulsion forces increase rapidly due to internuclear repulsion, causing a sharp spike in energy
## II. Classification of Chemical Species and Forces
### A. Terminology of Matter
#### Element vs. Compound
##### a.  Element: Contains the same type of atoms (e.g., Diamond, O2)
##### b.  Compound: Contains different types of atoms (e.g., CO2, SiO2)
#### Molecule vs. Lattice
##### a.  Molecule: A group of atoms with independent existence (e.g., H2O, CO2)
##### b.  Lattice: Repeated units arranged in a uniform manner without independent existence (e.g., NaCl, Diamond, SiO2)
### B. Strength of Attraction Forces
#### Strong Forces (Chemical Bonds)
##### a.  Definition: Inter-atomic forces where energy release is > 42 kJ/mol (10 kcal/mol)
##### b.  Types: Ionic, Covalent, Coordinate, and Metallic bonds
##### c.  Nature: Involves bonded atoms held tightly together
#### Weak Forces (Intermolecular Forces)
##### a.  Definition: Forces between molecules (or non-bonded atoms) where energy release is ≤ 42 kJ/mol
##### b.  Types: Van der Waals forces and Hydrogen bonds
##### c.  Context: Occurs between independent molecules (e.g., between two Cl2 molecules) rather than atoms within a molecule
## III. Ionic Bonding (Electrovalent Bond)
### A. Formation and Definition
#### Electron Transfer
##### a.  Formed by the complete transfer of electrons from one atom (usually a metal) to another (usually a non-metal)
##### b.  Results in the formation of cations (positive) and anions (negative)
#### Electrostatic Attraction
##### a.  The bond is the electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions
##### b.  It is non-directional (omni-directional), meaning the field extends in all directions
### B. Conditions for Formation
#### Energy Balance
##### a.  Total released energy (Lattice Energy + Electron Affinity) must exceed total required energy (Ionization Energy)
##### b.  Low Ionization Energy: Required for the metal to easily lose electrons
##### c.  High Electron Affinity: Required for the non-metal to readily accept electrons
##### d.  High Lattice Energy: Released when ions combine to form a solid lattice
### C. Properties of Ionic Compounds
#### Physical State
##### a.  Exist as solids due to strong electrostatic forces packing ions closely
##### b.  Have high melting and boiling points
#### Brittleness
##### a.  Ionic solids are brittle; applying force causes like charges to slide next to each other, leading to repulsion and fracture
#### Isomerism
##### a.  Do not show stereoisomerism because the bond is non-directional (3D arrangement is uniform)
## IV. Covalent Bonding Theories
### A. Lewis Octet Rule
#### The Principle
##### a.  Atoms react to complete their outer shell with 8 electrons (octet) to achieve inert gas configuration
##### b.  Achieved via transfer (Ionic) or sharing (Covalent/Coordinate) of electrons
#### Exceptions and Failures
##### a.  Hypovalent Species: Molecules with < 8 valence electrons (e.g., BeCl2, BF3)
##### b.  Hypervalent Species: Molecules with > 8 valence electrons (e.g., PCl5, SF6), utilizing d-orbitals
##### c.  Odd Electron Species: Molecules with an odd number of total electrons (e.g., NO, NO2) which cannot fully pair
##### d.  Noble Gas Compounds: The existence of Xenon compounds disproved the idea that noble gas configurations are inert/unreactive
### B. Valence Bond Theory (VBT) & Overlap
#### Orbital Overlap Concept
##### a.  Covalent bonds form via the overlap of half-filled atomic orbitals containing electrons with opposite spins
##### b.  Overlap implies a common area where electron density is shared
#### Types of Overlap (Bond Types)
##### a.  Sigma ($\sigma$) Bond: Formed by head-on (axial) overlap along the internuclear axis (e.g., s-s, s-p, p-p axial)
##### b.  Pi ($\pi$) Bond: Formed by sideways (collateral) overlap perpendicular to the internuclear axis
##### c.  Delta ($\delta$) Bond: Formed by the overlap of four lobes (e.g., d-d face-to-face)
#### Strength of Overlap
##### a.  Bond Type: $\sigma$ > $\pi$ > $\delta$ (Sigma is stronger due to greater extent of overlap along the axis)
##### b.  Shell Number: Lower shell number (smaller size) leads to stronger overlap (e.g., 2p-2p > 3p-3p)
##### c.  Directional Nature: Directional orbitals (p, d) form stronger bonds than non-directional (s) orbitals (e.g., 2p-2p > 2s-2s)
## V. Hybridization
### A. Concept and Necessity
#### Need for Theory
##### a.  Simple overlap (VBT) failed to explain bond angles in molecules like CH4 (experimentally 109°28', not 90°)
##### b.  Explains the equivalence of bonds (bond energy and length) in molecules like CH4
#### Process
##### a.  Intermixing of atomic orbitals of slightly different energies to form new "hybrid" orbitals of equivalent energy and shape
##### b.  The number of hybrid orbitals formed equals the number of atomic orbitals mixed
### B. Types and Geometries
#### sp Hybridization
  - Mixing of one s and one p; Linear geometry (180°)
#### sp² Hybridization
  - Mixing of one s and two p; Trigonal Planar geometry (120°)
#### sp³ Hybridization
  - Mixing of one s and three p; Tetrahedral geometry (109°28')
#### sp³d Hybridization
  - Trigonal Bipyramidal (TBP); contains Equatorial (120°) and Axial (90°) positions
#### sp³d² Hybridization
  - Octahedral (Square Bipyramidal); all angles 90°
#### sp³d³ Hybridization
  - Pentagonal Bipyramidal; angles 72° and 90°
### C. Drago's Rule (No Hybridization)
#### Conditions
  - Central atom from 3rd period or below, bonded to substituents with EN $\le$ 2.1 (like H), and has lone pairs
#### Outcome
  - Pure p-orbitals participate in bonding; bond angles remain close to 90° (e.g., PH3, H2S) rather than the tetrahedral angle
## VI. VSEPR Theory and Bent's Rule
### A. Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR)
#### Repulsion Orders
##### a.  Lone Pair-Lone Pair > Lone Pair-Bond Pair > Bond Pair-Bond Pair
##### b.  Triple Bond > Double Bond > Single Bond repulsions
#### Impact on Shape
##### a.  Electronic Geometry: Arrangement of all electron pairs (includes lone pairs)
##### b.  Molecular Geometry (Shape): Arrangement of atoms only (excludes lone pairs)
##### c.  Example: NH3 has tetrahedral electronic geometry but pyramidal shape due to one lone pair
### B. Bent's Rule
#### Percent s-Character
##### a.  Lone pairs occupy orbitals with higher s-character (closer to nucleus, more space)
##### b.  More electronegative substituents occupy orbitals with less s-character (more p-character)
#### Application in TBP (sp³d)
##### a.  Lone pairs prefer Equatorial positions (more s-character, larger angle)
##### b.  Electronegative atoms (like F in PCl3F2) prefer Axial positions (less s-character)
## VII. Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)
### A. Formation of Molecular Orbitals
#### Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO)
##### a.  Constructive Interference: ($\Psi_A + \Psi_B$) leads to Bonding Molecular Orbitals (BMO) with increased electron density between nuclei
##### b.  Destructive Interference: ($\Psi_A - \Psi_B$) leads to Anti-bonding Molecular Orbitals (ABMO) with a node between nuclei
#### Energy Relationship
##### a.  BMO has lower energy (more stable) than parent atomic orbitals
##### b.  ABMO has higher energy (less stable) than parent atomic orbitals
### B. Mixing and Diagrams
#### s-p Mixing
##### a.  Occurs when energy gap between 2s and 2p is small (Total electrons $\le$ 14, e.g., B2, C2, N2)
##### b.  Result: $\sigma_{2p}$ orbital is higher in energy than $\pi_{2p}$ orbitals
#### No s-p Mixing
##### a.  Occurs when energy gap is large (Total electrons > 14, e.g., O2, F2)
##### b.  Result: $\sigma_{2p}$ is lower in energy than $\pi_{2p}$
### C. Applications
#### Bond Order
  - $0.5 \times (N_b - N_a)$. Used to determine bond stability and existence
##### a.  Example: He2 has bond order 0 (does not exist)
#### Magnetic Behavior
  - Unpaired electrons in MOs indicate paramagnetism (e.g., O2 is paramagnetic due to unpaired electrons in $\pi^*$ orbitals)
#### HOMO/LUMO
  - Highest Occupied and Lowest Unoccupied MOs used to explain color transitions (e.g., Halogens)
## VIII. Resonance and Back Bonding
### A. Resonance
#### Delocalization
  - It is the delocalization of $\pi$ (pi) electrons within a molecule
#### Reason
  - A single Lewis structure cannot explain all properties (bond lengths, energies) of a molecule (e.g., Benzene, Carbonate ion)
#### Bond Order Calculation
  - Average bond order = (Total bonds in stable structures) / (Number of resonating structures)
### B. Back Bonding
#### Definition
  - A type of partial $\pi$-bond formed by the donation of a lone pair from one atom to a vacant orbital of an adjacent atom
#### Conditions
##### a.  One atom must have a lone pair, the other a vacant orbital
##### b.  At least one of the atoms must be from the 2nd period (small size required for effective sideways overlap)
#### Examples
##### a.  BF3: F donates lone pair to vacant p-orbital of B (2p$\pi$-2p$\pi$)
##### b.  N(SiH3)3: N donates lone pair to vacant d-orbital of Si (2p$\pi$-3d$\pi$), making the molecule planar
## IX. Polarization (Fajan’s Rule) & Solubility
### A. Polarization Concept
#### Distortion
  - The distortion of the anion's electron cloud by the cation's positive charge
#### Covalent Character
  - Polarization introduces covalent character into ionic bonds
### B. Fajan's Rules (Factors)
#### Cation Size
  - Smaller cation $\rightarrow$ higher charge density $\rightarrow$ high polarizing power
#### Anion Size
  - Larger anion $\rightarrow$ outer electrons held loosely $\rightarrow$ high polarizability
#### Charge
  - Higher charge on either ion increases polarization
#### Configuration
  - Cations with pseudo-inert gas configuration (nd10, e.g., Cu+, Ag+) polarize more than inert gas configuration (ns2np6) due to poor shielding by d-electrons
### C. Applications
#### Solubility
  - High polarization $\rightarrow$ high covalent character $\rightarrow$ lower solubility in polar solvents
#### Color
  - Polarization causes charge transfer spectra, imparting color to compounds (e.g., AgI is yellow, AgCl is white)
#### Thermal Stability
  - For polyatomic anions (carbonates), high polarization by the cation weakens the C-O bond, lowering thermal stability
## X. Specific Chemistries (Oxyacids, Silicates, Silicones)
### A. Oxyacids
#### Parent Acids
  - Suffix "-ic" (e.g., Sulfuric, Phosphoric)
#### Derivatives
##### a.  Removal of oxygen $\rightarrow$ "-ous" acid
##### b.  Removal of water from 2 moles of acid $\rightarrow$ "Pyro-" acid
##### c.  Removal of 1 mole water from 1 mole acid $\rightarrow$ "Meta-" acid
### B. Silicates
#### Basic Unit
  - SiO4 tetrahedron ($sp^3$ hybridised Si)
#### Classification based on Oxygen Sharing
##### a.  Orthosilicate: 0 oxygens shared (discrete SiO4 units)
##### b.  Pyrosilicate: 1 oxygen shared between two units
##### c.  Chain Silicates: 2 oxygens shared (single chain)
##### d.  Sheet Silicates: 3 oxygens shared (2D sheet)
##### e.  3D Silicates: 4 oxygens shared (e.g., Quartz)
### C. Silicones
#### Structure
  - Organo-polymeric silicones containing R2SiO repeating units
#### Properties
  - Hydrophobic (water-repellent) due to alkyl groups, high thermal stability
