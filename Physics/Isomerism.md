# Isomerism

#### Here is an ultra-detailed hierarchical outline of Isomerism based on the provided sources.
## I. Fundamental Concepts of Isomerism
### A. Definition and Analogy
#### 1. Basic Definition
##### a. Isomerism arises when compounds have the same molecular formula but exist in different forms
##### b. It involves changes in the relationship between atoms within the same formula
#### 2. Real-life Analogya. The lecturer uses the human body as a "molecular formula" where movement creates different isomers
##### b. Moving limbs creates different arrangements, while detaching and reattaching a limb creates different connectivity
### B. Classification Basis
#### 1. Two Main Categoriesa. Connectivity: Refers to which atom is bonded to which
##### b. Arrangement: Refers to the spatial orientation (e.g., wedge/dash) without breaking bonds
#### 2. Priority Rule
##### a. If both arrangement and connectivity change, connectivity is considered first
##### b. Compounds with different connectivity are Structural Isomers; those with only different arrangement are Stereoisomers
## II. Structural Isomerism
### A. Types of Structural Isomers
#### 1. Chain Isomerism
##### a. Same formula and functional group, but different parent carbon chain or side chain
##### b. Can involve linear versus branched structures
#### 2. Position Isomerism
##### a. Same formula and parent chain, but different positions of substituents, multiple bonds, or functional groups
##### b. Example: 1-chlorobutane vs. 2-chlorobutane
#### 3. Functional Isomerism
##### a. Same formula but different functional groups
##### b. Examples include Alcohol vs. Ether, or Aldehyde vs. Ketone
#### 4. Ring-Chain Isomerism
##### a. One isomer is an open chain while the other contains a ring
##### b. Often grouped with functional isomerism, but "Ring-Chain" is the specific priority term if both options exist
#### 5. Metamerisma. Requires a polyvalent functional group (valency > 1) like ether, ketone, or secondary amine
##### b. Arises from a change in the carbon chain/skeleton on either side of the polyvalent group
##### c. Priority rule: Metamerism generally takes precedence over position isomerism
### B. Calculation of Structural Isomers
#### 1. Degree of Unsaturation (DU)
##### a. Used to determine the presence of rings or double bonds
##### b. DU = 0 implies no double bonds or rings (saturated acyclic)
#### 2. Methodologya. Start with the longest carbon chain and systematically reduce chain length while adding branches
##### b. Check for symmetry to avoid counting identical structures (e.g., "Mercedes" shape vs. linear)
## III. Tautomerism
### A. Definition and Mechanism
#### 1. Concept
##### a. Also known as Desmotropy (bond turning) or oscillation of atoms
##### b. Involves the migration of a proton ($H^+$) and the shifting of pi-bond electrons
##### c. It is a real phenomenon occurring in dynamic equilibrium, unlike resonance which is hypothetical
#### 2. Mechanism (Triad System)
##### a. Occurs between three atoms (e.g., Carbon-Carbon-Oxygen)
##### b. Process: Remove $H^+$ (acidic alpha-H), form anion, perform resonance, and attach $H^+$ to the electronegative atom
##### c. Requires an alpha-carbon with hybridization capable of resonance (usually $sp^3$)
### B. Keto-Enol Tautomerism
#### 1. Stability Factors
##### a. Generally, the Keto form is more stable due to the high bond energy of the C=O bond
##### b. The Enol form dominates only if specific stabilizing factors exist
#### 2. Factors Favoring Enol Content
##### a. Aromaticity: If enolization creates an aromatic ring (e.g., Phenol), it is highly stable
##### b. Hydrogen Bonding: Intramolecular H-bonding stabilizes the enol
##### c. Conjugation/Repulsion Relief: In 1,3-dicarbonyls, enolization relieves dipole repulsion ("Tan Tana Tan") and creates conjugation
### C. Deuterium Exchange
#### 1. Process
##### a. Treating a ketone/aldehyde with $OD^-/D_2O$ leads to the replacement of active alpha-hydrogens with Deuterium (D)
##### b. The number of exchanged D atoms equals the number of acidic alpha-hydrogens involved in resonance
#### 2. Counting Method
##### a. Identify all positions where the negative charge can delocalize during tautomerism
##### b. Count the total hydrogens on those specific carbons
## IV. Geometrical Isomerism (GI)
### A. Fundamentals
#### 1. Definitiona. Isomers arising from restricted rotation systems (RRS) leading to different spatial distances between groups
##### b. Also called "Diastereomers" in the broader sense as they are not mirror images
#### 2. Conditions for GIa. Must have a Restricted Rotation System (Double Bond or Ring)
##### b. Terminals ($T_1$ and $T_2$) must each have two different groups attached ($A \neq B$)
##### c. The relative distance between groups on $T_1$ and $T_2$ must change upon permutation
### B. Systems Showing GI
#### 1. Alkenes and Heterodouble Bonds
##### a. C=C, C=N (Oximes), and N=N (Azo) systems can show GI
##### b. C=O does not show GI because Oxygen has two identical lone pairs
#### 2. Cycloalkanes
##### a. A ring acts as an RRS
##### b. Requires at least two different $sp^3$ carbons (terminals) with different groups attached
#### 3. Cumulenes and Allenes
##### a. Even number of cumulative double bonds leads to perpendicular orbitals (No GI due to equidistant groups)
##### b. Odd number of cumulative double bonds creates a planar system, allowing GI
#### 4. Spiranes and Biphenyls
##### a. Even number of rings in spiranes creates perpendicular planes (No GI)
##### b. Odd number of rings or appropriate substitution in biphenyls (to prevent rotation) allows GI
#### 5. Cycloalkenes
##### a. Double bonds inside rings smaller than 8 carbons do not show GI (only Cis exists)
##### b. Rings with 8 or more carbons can accommodate Trans forms
### C. Nomenclature of GI
#### 1. Cis-Transa. Cis: Identical groups on the same side
##### b. Trans: Identical groups on opposite sides
#### 2. E-Z System (CIP Rules)
##### a. Used when all four groups are different
##### b. Based on atomic number of directly attached atoms (Cahn-Ingold-Prelog rules)
##### c. Z (Zusammen): High priority groups on the same side
##### d. E (Entgegen): High priority groups on opposite sides
#### 3. Syn-Anti
##### a. Used primarily for oximes and azo compounds
## V. Conformational Isomerism
### A. Nature of Conformers
#### 1. Rotation
##### a. Arises from free rotation around Sigma ($\sigma$) bonds
##### b. Infinite conformations are possible
#### 2. Projectionsa. Newman Projection: Front carbon is a dot; rear carbon is a circle
##### b. Sawhorse Projection: Side view of the bond
### B. Conformational Analysis of Alkanes
#### 1. Ethane
##### a. Eclipsed: High energy, maximum repulsion (torsional strain)
##### b. Staggered: Low energy, minimum repulsion
##### c. Energy barrier is approximately 12.5 kJ/mol
#### 2. Butane
##### a. Anti: Methyl groups 180° apart (Most stable)
##### b. Gauche: Methyl groups 60° apart
##### c. Partially Eclipsed: Methyl eclipses Hydrogen
##### d. Fully Eclipsed: Methyl eclipses Methyl (Least stable)
#### 3. Stability Order
##### a. Generally: Anti > Gauche > Partially Eclipsed > Fully Eclipsed
##### b. Gauche Effect: In cases involving H-bonding (e.g., glycol) or ionic attraction, Gauche becomes more stable than Anti
### C. Cyclohexane Conformations
#### 1. Chair Forma. Most stable form with virtually no angle or torsional strain
##### b. Bonds are classified as Axial (vertical) or Equatorial (horizontal/slanted)
##### c. Bulky groups prefer the equatorial position to avoid 1,3-diaxial interaction (Flagpole repulsion)
#### 2. Other Forms
##### a. Boat: Less stable due to flagpole repulsion between hydrogens
##### b. Twist Boat: Slightly more stable than pure boat
##### c. Half Chair: Highly unstable transition state
#### 3. Stability Order
##### a. Chair > Twist Boat > Boat > Half Chair
## VI. Optical Isomerism
### A. Symmetry Elements
#### 1. Plane of Symmetry (POS)
##### a. An imaginary plane bisecting the molecule into mirror-image halves
##### b. Includes "Slice" cuts through planar molecules (e.g., benzene)
#### 2. Center of Symmetry (COS)
##### a. A point from which extending lines in opposite directions encounters identical groups at equal distances
##### b. Not present in Tetrahedral structures; requires specific geometry like trans-dimers
#### 3. Axis of Symmetry (AOS) & AAOS
##### a. AOS involves rotation by $360/n$ degrees to yield an indistinguishable structure
##### b. Alternating Axis (AAOS) involves rotation followed by reflection
### B. Polarimetry and Optical Activity
#### 1. The Experiment
##### a. Plane Polarized Light (PPL) is passed through a sample tube
##### b. Optically Active: Rotates PPL (Chiral)
##### c. Optically Inactive: Does not rotate PPL (Achiral)
#### 2. Measurement
##### a. Dextrorotatory (d/+): Rotates light clockwise
##### b. Levorotatory (l/-): Rotates light anti-clockwise
##### c. Specific Rotation Formula: $\alpha = \theta_{obs} / (l \times c)$, where $l$ is length in dm and $c$ is concentration in g/mL
### C. Chirality and Nomenclature
#### 1. Chiral Center
##### a. An $sp^3$ atom attached to four different groups
##### b. Not synonymous with a Chiral Molecule; a molecule can have chiral centers but be inactive (Meso)
#### 2. Projections
##### a. Fischer Projection: Vertical lines are away (Dash), horizontal lines are towards (Wedge)
##### b. Wedge-Dash: Wedge is towards viewer, Dash is away
#### 3. Nomenclature Systems
##### a. D/L System: Relative configuration based on the position of -OH or -NH2 on the penultimate carbon (Right=D, Left=L)
##### b. R/S System: Absolute configuration based on CIP priority
  - i. 1 $\to$ 2 $\to$ 3 Clockwise = R; Anti-clockwise = S
  - ii. Rule flips if the lowest priority group is on the horizontal line (Fischer) or Wedge (3D)
##### c. Erythro/Threo: Erythro (groups on same side), Threo (groups on opposite sides)
### D. Stereochemical Relationships
#### 1. Enantiomers
##### a. Non-superimposable mirror images
##### b. Must be optically active molecules
##### c. Have identical physical properties except for interaction with PPL
#### 2. Diastereomers
##### a. Stereoisomers that are not mirror images
##### b. Have different physical properties
#### 3. Meso Compounds
##### a. Achiral compounds possessing chiral centers
##### b. Optically inactive due to internal compensation (symmetry)
#### 4. Racemic Mixturea. Equimolar mixture of Enantiomers (50% d + 50% l) 95, 94.b. Net optical rotation is zero due to external compensation
## VII. Calculation of Stereoisomers
### A. Unsymmetric Molecules
#### 1. Formula
##### a. Total stereoisomers = $2^n$, where $n$ is the number of stereogenic units (chiral centers + GI sites)
#### 2. Application
##### a. Apply purely when there is no symmetry in the molecule
### B. Symmetric Molecules
#### 1. Adjustments
##### a. The $2^n$ formula is invalid due to Meso compounds or identical isomers
##### b. Calculations involve breaking down into active forms and meso forms based on odd/even values of $n$ (though the source emphasizes manual counting/logic over complex formulas)
#### 2. Pseudo-Chiral Centers
##### a. Centers that are chiral only under specific configurations of neighbors (e.g., r/s neighbors) 100, 101.b. Must be accounted for in rigorous counting
